The governance literature prescribes upward flow almost everywhere. However, "feedback" means three different things in it. In the first sense, the system learns from its users: "Once humans use GenAI, the system will learn from usage, resulting in feedback loops", and "as the system evolves, governance should also evolve and consider the new situation" [@janssen2025responsiblegenai, p. 44]. In the second sense, regulation revises itself. For example, @taeihagh2025govgenai [p. 7] calls for "an adaptive approach that facilitates iteratively responding to new risks and challenges swiftly", a demand addressed to regulators rather than to organisations. Only the third sense concerns people raising matters with those who decide the rules. In data governance, "an escalation process helps to address issues to higher levels of authority", which "enables stakeholders to give feedback, e.g. concerning policy changes to meet new business requirements" [@abraham2019datagovframework, p. 430]. Furthermore, the later review by @responsibleaigovreview [p. 14] asks organisations to "empower their employees to raise questions or concerns about AI systems". Frameworks written for generative AI go further. They combine "top-down policies and bottom-up feedback" [@joshi2025resai, sec. IV.C] and ask that individuals be able to "report concerns or initiate changes themselves" [@joshi2025resai, sec. IV.D], with "feedback loops and iteration mechanisms" built into every phase of adoption [@weinberg2025faigmoe, p. 9]. However, these last three are preprints. Among peer-reviewed sources, the route from practitioners to rule-setters rests on one data governance review and one call to empower employees. In short, all of it is prescribed.

Where a feedback loop has been observed, it adjusts a system or an instrument, not the rules for its use. For example, at a public agency's machine learning lab, @asatiani2020blackbox [p. 275] found periodic reviews "collecting feedback from application users and from data scientists on the algorithms' operation, with the functionality being adjusted accordingly". Caseworkers could also change the thresholds at which the applications flagged cases, and a team leader called "the ability to mute a model or change the threshold" a major cultural factor in the business's adoption of the technology [-@asatiani2020blackbox, p. 273]. Even there, a data scientist reported that "the difficult part has been to get the dialogue with the case workers" [-@asatiani2020blackbox, p. 274]. In a preprint case study, practitioners' complaint that the risk questions "seemed excessive" led the researchers to revise their instrument [@lee2024questionbank, p. 14]. Both loops are real, and both are local. What changed was an application's behaviour or a questionnaire, and nothing in either account travelled to whoever set the organisation's rules. Furthermore, the public-sector case predates generative AI and concerns systems the agency built itself. Its loop therefore runs between the builders and the users of one application. In contrast, a usage rule for a generative AI tool that the organisation buys rather than builds involves no such pair inside one project. In my view, the observed loops thus say nothing about how the people bound by such a rule might be heard.

Where studies look for a channel between practitioners and rule-setters, they find none described, or find it treated as a low priority. First, the 2025 review names the gap directly, noting a "lack of research identifying how rights and responsibilities should be assigned for vertical decision-making" [@responsibleaigovreview, p. 11]. Second, the one field study of non-champions, a preprint accepted at CHI 2026, describes governance champions who designed the governance structures while many data scientists remained disengaged, and it describes no route by which the data scientists' views reached the designers [@stickystories2025, p. 3]. Third, in a small preprint survey of 44 professionals recruited through professional networks and LinkedIn, stakeholder engagement was "the least selected option" among the parts of a responsible AI framework that respondents thought needed strengthening [@agenticaiperceptions2025, pp. 4, 14]. Finally, the one study that observes unauthorised AI use recommends governance committees that "adapt policies dynamically based on frontline tool adoption and feedback loops", but it measures no such channel [@silic2025shadowai, p. 12]. In my view, the absence is partly an absence of research and partly an absence in the organisations studied, and the literature does not allow the two to be separated.

Where upward change has been observed, the people who carried it had chosen to. In the interviews of @rakova2021practitionerperspectives [p. 7:11], a full-time responsible AI team was in several cases "only catalyzed by the results from volunteer-led investigations of potential bias issues", and in a few cases champions' "grassroots actions and internal advocacy with leadership" made responsible AI a company-wide priority. Thus, the authors conclude that "individuals rather than organizational processes or structures remain the engine of proactive practices" [-@rakova2021practitionerperspectives, p. 7:11]. Furthermore, being heard depended on rank. Several practitioners said their concerns "were heard on account of their seniority", while a data scientist volunteering with the responsible AI team, who saw ethical concerns, found that "People weren't open for scrutinization" [-@rakova2021practitionerperspectives, p. 7:14]. The authors read this as a sign of fragility, since accountability then "relies on the individual's own resources, interests, and situational power rather than scalable and systemic organizational structures" [-@rakova2021practitionerperspectives, p. 7:14]. In short, upward change on this evidence is the work of committed individuals with standing, not of a channel. However, the evidence is about responsible AI initiatives, which the participants had taken on, rather than about rules imposed on them.

Organisation theory offers explanations of why concerns do or do not travel, although none of the three bodies of work concerns AI rules. The first is the literature on employee voice. @morrison2023voicesilence [p. 80] restricts voice to "internal and upward voice behavior", that is, speaking up to someone higher in the organisation. Her review finds that employees are more likely to speak up "when they perceive that doing so will be safe and effective" [-@morrison2023voicesilence, p. 85]. Two findings she reports bear directly on what comes back. First, when supervisors give no explanation for not implementing a suggestion, employees see voice as less safe and speak up less, while a sensitive explanation makes later voice more likely [-@morrison2023voicesilence, p. 90]. Second, formal voice mechanisms "might cause employees to be selective in what they express", so that employees share helpful ideas while withholding disruptive ones [-@morrison2023voicesilence, p. 94]. She also calls for research on how informal voice is affected "by the presence or absence of formal voice channels" [-@morrison2023voicesilence, p. 99]. The second body of work is issue selling. @dutton1993issueselling [p. 398] add a strategic reading, in which issue selling is "individuals' behaviors that are directed toward affecting others' attention to and understanding of issues". Their propositions, written about middle managers, predict more selling "when top management is perceived as more supportive and open" [-@dutton1993issueselling, p. 409] and when the seller "can identify a solution to attach to the issue" [-@dutton1993issueselling, p. 410]. They also warn that framing an issue as top management's responsibility makes it likelier that the seller "will lose credibility for future selling attempts" [-@dutton1993issueselling, p. 414]. However, their model ends at attention. It treats attention as "a necessary precursor" to substantive action and deliberately leaves the step to action unexamined [-@dutton1993issueselling, pp. 404, 421]. The third is sensegiving, the attempt to influence how others make sense of a change [@gioia1991sensemaking, p. 442], which Section 2.6 takes up as part of its second lens. In the study that introduced the term, "the sensegiving activities of the various stakeholders also leads to some modifications to the espoused vision" [@gioia1991sensemaking, p. 443]. However, the change there was led from the top, and the authors frame it around "the CEO's primary role in instigating the strategic change process" [-@gioia1991sensemaking, p. 433]. Furthermore, @rouleau2005micropractices [p. 1435] relates two of the practices by which middle managers give sense to issue selling. However, in her case these practices were aimed at the firm's clients, not at senior managers [-@rouleau2005micropractices, pp. 1428–1429]. Thus, in both studies sensegiving from below answers a change that others started, which is not the same as raising a concern about a rule.

Together, these literatures leave SQ2 with questions rather than expectations. First, does anything leave the BI practitioner's level at all, and if it does, how far does it travel? Second, what comes back? It could be a yes or a no, an explanation, a new rule, or nothing. Third, if nothing went up, was there no route, or a route that people chose not to use? If people chose not to use a route, did their reasons resemble the safety and efficacy judgements Morrison describes? These questions are connected. On Morrison's account, an unexplained refusal makes the next concern less likely to be raised. Thus, what comes back from one request may decide whether a second is ever made. Moreover, whether issue selling, theorised for middle managers, applies to analysts without managerial standing is itself open. I argue that a finding that nothing travels would answer these questions as fully as a finding that something does, because the literature prescribes the route but has almost never looked for it.
