## 2.1 How does the literature assume rules arrive?

The literature defines organisational AI governance as a means of regulating conduct. @mantymaki2022definingaigov [pp. 604–605] call it "a system of rules, practices, processes, and technological tools that are employed to ensure an organization's use of AI technologies aligns with the organization's strategies, objectives, and values; fulfills legal requirements; and meets principles of ethical AI followed by the organization". They describe its elements as "methods of regulating behavior to keep it within acceptable boundaries and enable desirable behavior" [@mantymaki2022definingaigov, p. 605]. @birkstedt2023themesgaps [p. 135] build their review on the same four elements. Data governance, the source of much of this vocabulary, is framed in parallel: it "formalizes data policies, standards, and procedures and monitors compliance" [@abraham2019datagovframework, p. 426]. These definitions are conceptual, not reports of observed practice, but they set what the rest of the literature means by a rule. In this study, a *rule* is any expectation, written or unwritten, about what BI practitioners may or may not do with generative AI, however it reaches them: a document, a ruling on a request, a blocked tool or a colleague's advice.

The literature answers the question of where rules come from with a hierarchy that places rule-making above the practitioner. In general and data governance, corporate governance "sets the principles through which organizations interpret and enact the desirable behavior of their agents" [@mantymaki2022definingaigov, p. 605]; data governance adds reporting structures, governance bodies and a hierarchical level at which decision authority sits [@abraham2019datagovframework, pp. 428–429]; pattern catalogues spread governance across industry, organisation and team levels, with an ethics committee that approves and monitors projects [@lu2024raipatterns, pp. 173:6, 173:10]; and enterprise models stack a strategy tier above organisation and lifecycle-management tiers [@ismail2025frameworks, p. 129]. Frameworks written for generative AI state the cascade outright. Operational staff "follow governance policies set at the strategic level" [@joshi2025resai, sec. IV.A], and risk questions run from the broadest, addressed to C-level decision-makers, to the most detailed, addressed to practitioners [@lee2024questionbank, p. 8]. Practitioners, in turn, appear as the object of governance, filed under "who is being governed" [@batool2024aigov, p. 3; @batool2024rai, sec. 3.1]. They implement ethics while "the parameters are set by legislation, organizational norms and clients", and a power imbalance separates those who set specifications from those who implement them [Orr & Davis, 2020, as cited in @birkstedt2023themesgaps, p. 147]. The cascade is prescribed, not observed: of the 68 papers reviewed by @birkstedt2023themesgaps [p. 138], 61 were conceptual and three empirical.

The literature treats the way rules reach people lower down as a matter of channel design. A code of ethics is "a set of rules that employees should uphold when developing an AI system" [@lu2024raipatterns, p. 173:10]. Communication is meant to keep stakeholders continuously aware, and training helps them act according to data policies, processes and procedures [@abraham2019datagovframework, p. 430]. For generative AI, @joshi2025resai [sec. IV.D] give literacy programmes the job of preventing staff from "uploading confidential content into GenAI tools like ChatGPT". Only @tallon2013informationartifact offer fieldwork on this point, and it comes from the top of the organisation. Their executives described user education that explains "why governance policies are written in specific ways so that end users do not try to trivialize, circumvent, or ignore the rules" [@tallon2013informationartifact, p. 165]. The authors concede that "our interviews involved IT executives rather than users", so they could not locate the point at which governance starts to burden the people it governs [@tallon2013informationartifact, p. 168]. The review by @birkstedt2023themesgaps [p. 153] concludes that organisations rely on published ethics documents and training, "but their effectiveness is uncertain". The channel is designed and delivery is assumed. Whether a delivered rule is read, and whether it is the rule a practitioner acts on, these sources do not examine.

In this literature a rule is a text, arranged in layers and stages. Procedural mechanisms comprise a strategy, policies, standards, processes and procedures [@abraham2019datagovframework, p. 429]. Responsible AI governance adds documentation duties, such as developers documenting the data life cycle [@responsibleaigovreview, p. 9], and procedural practices that "concern a series of actions at different levels" [@responsibleaigovreview, p. 10]. Preprint frameworks extend the layering. The five layers of @agarwal2025fivelayer [p. 2] offer "increasingly detailed guidance as one moves down the layers", although the authors explicitly leave corporate governance outside their scope. @luna2024paradigms break generative AI governance into processes and sub-processes. @joshi2025resai [sec. IV.E] make each lifecycle phase "a governance checkpoint". A rule conceived this way has a date, an author and a place in a hierarchy of documents. Whether the people it governs can say when it reached them, or who issued it, is not a question these sources ask.

Above the organisation, the layers differ by jurisdiction. In a review of 21 policy and academic documents, @ismail2025frameworks [p. 127] contrast the EU AI Act, which sorts systems into four risk classes with binding obligations, with the voluntary ASEAN guide [-@ismail2025frameworks, p. 128]. Without an enforceable common standard, businesses operating in several countries "face unclear obligations and inconsistent oversight" [@ismail2025frameworks, p. 131]. The comparison by @luna2024paradigms finds that risk-based regimes cover far more governance processes than principle- or outcome-based ones. The work of translation falls to the organisation, which "must develop appropriate mechanisms to identify external stimuli quickly and accurately and interpret how they affect their AI applications" [@responsibleaigovreview, p. 10]. Jurisdiction therefore adds a level to the cascade without changing its direction: regulation constrains the organisation, and the organisation's rules constrain its staff.

Beneath the tiers, channels and layers lies an assumption about sequence: governance comes first. @ismail2025frameworks [p. 128] commend anticipatory governance, in which ethical risks are assessed "well in advance during the design stage". @lu2024raipatterns [p. 173:18] recommend assessing a system's behaviour "before deploying AI systems in the real world". Responsible AI governance is defined as ensuring appropriate functionality "over the entire lifecycle" [@responsibleaigovreview, p. 6], and @weinberg2025faigmoe [p. 10] states the principle plainly: governance frameworks "are established before widespread deployment". The object of these lifecycles is an AI system that an organisation develops or procures and then deploys. A generative AI assistant open in a browser passes through no such gate, and it may be in use before any rule is written. There is one doubt from inside the literature: @responsibleaigovreview [p. 7] warn that "a principles-first approach may prove counterproductive and incompatible with the organizational modus operandi". There is also one observed counterpoint. The interviewees of @rakova2021practitionerperspectives [p. 7:15], people who had taken on responsible AI work, reported that information spread through individual relationships and that they relied on personal ties to navigate "obscured organizational structures". Taken together, the models assume that the rule comes first and arrives as text. They leave open what happens when a rule lands on practice already under way, and through whom it actually travels. Section 2.2 turns to the literature on practice that runs ahead of rules.
