## 2.6 What lens does the literature offer, and what does it leave unstudied?

The literature offers one typology that recurs from IT governance to AI governance. @tallon2013informationartifact [p. 142], interviewing 37 executives in 30 organisations, found "a range of structural, procedural, and relational practices used to govern information". @abraham2019datagovframework [p. 428] use the same division for data governance, and in a field study of three firms @papagiannidis2023towardaigov [p. 125] adopt it as "the main dimensions" of AI governance, building their model around it [-@papagiannidis2023towardaigov, p. 135]. The later review by @responsibleaigovreview [p. 10] defines the three: structural practices assign "roles and responsibilities for decision-making", procedural practices concern how governance is executed, and relational practices concern "the different links among employees within and outside the organization". This study uses the typology as a lens rather than as a fixed set of codes. The distinction lets an account of a rule be read three ways: who decided it, how it was meant to be carried out, and through whom it actually travelled. The same review asks for exactly the third reading, arguing that relational practices "should also be examined through the informal channels through which they are diffused and executed" [@responsibleaigovreview, p. 14].

The first gap is the absence of the governed. Of the 36 governance sources screened for this review, 28 or 29 (78–81%) contain no account of how staff below the rule-setting level receive AI governance; the range reflects one study of information governance whose interviews with executives report users' behaviour at second hand (Section 3.7). The authors themselves name the gap. @birkstedt2023themesgaps [p. 158] call for research on "how individuals enact AIG practices", the review by @responsibleaigovreview [p. 14] finds that "the role of the individual within an organization has been largely overlooked", and @mantymaki2022definingaigov [p. 607] hold that "in-depth interviews and ethnographic studies can provide such understanding". Where fieldwork exists, it reaches staff at second hand. @tallon2013informationartifact [p. 168] note that "our interviews involved IT executives rather than users". In their field study, @papagiannidis2023towardaigov [p. 127] selected respondents with "a key position in the firm, for example, managers and leading developers", so their account of employees who "refused to use the new technologies" is reported by the developers who met that resistance [-@papagiannidis2023towardaigov, p. 131].

The second gap concerns who has been studied when practitioners are studied. @rakova2021practitionerperspectives [p. 7:7], the closest analogue, recruited interviewees whose work was in some aspect "related to the field of responsible AI"; 15 of their 26 participants held official responsible AI roles and 11 volunteered time for the work [-@rakova2021practitionerperspectives, p. 7:9]. They sampled governance's carriers. The one study of non-champions, a preprint accepted at CHI 2026, observed a single organisation in which mandated responsible AI templates were treated as "extra paperwork" [@stickystories2025, secs. 3.1–3.2]. Ordinary analysts who receive a rule restricting a tool they want to use sit between these two populations. Within this corpus, they have not been studied as recipients.

The third gap is that bypass has been surveyed, not traced. @silic2025shadowai [p. 2] surveyed "140 IT professionals and executives", with "roles ranging from analysts to senior managers" [-@silic2025shadowai, p. 6], and interviewed eight executives[^b9]. They identify a "governance drift zone", "a condition in which policies exist, but fail to align with the informal, emergent nature of AI tool use" [@silic2025shadowai, pp. 11–12]. The evidence that employees work around rules comes from the executives, who "recognized that Shadow AI cannot be fully controlled" [@silic2025shadowai, p. 10], and the authors concede that their findings "may underrepresent challenges faced by mid-level managers or employees" [@silic2025shadowai, p. 13]. A cross-sectional survey cannot show whether a given use began before any rule existed or continued around one, which is the distinction 2.3 left open.

The fourth gap is the setting. None of these field studies takes BI work as its unit of analysis: BI appears as dashboard tooling in the energy firms studied by @papagiannidis2023towardaigov [p. 127], and elsewhere not at all. This study therefore takes BI practitioners as recipients of their organisation's generative AI rules, relies on their own accounts, and anchors those accounts in specific incidents. The questions raised in 2.1 to 2.5 remain open questions for the data to answer, not claims this chapter has settled.

[^b9]: The abstract and introduction of @silic2025shadowai [pp. 1–2] report ten executive interviews; the methods, results and conclusion report eight [-@silic2025shadowai, pp. 6, 9, 11]. This review follows the body of the paper.
