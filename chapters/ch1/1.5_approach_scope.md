This study is qualitative and interpretive. Its evidence is BI practitioners' own accounts, gathered in semi-structured interviews and anchored in specific incidents of using or trying to use GenAI. Participants chose these incidents from a bounded menu, and each incident was traced through the same questions (Section 3.3) [PENDING: interviews Oct 5–19]. The design is an embedded multiple-case design with 14 BI practitioners in nine organisations in five countries. It has two depth cases with several participants each and seven breadth cases with one participant each, and the two strata are analysed separately and never pooled (Sections 3.1 and 3.2). Finally, the accounts are analysed by codebook thematic analysis. The structural, procedural and relational practices introduced in Section 2.6 serve as a starting frame rather than a set of expected answers (Section 3.4). Beside them, sensemaking, the process by which people turn circumstances into a situation they can put into words and act on, serves as a second, sensitising lens [@weick2005organizing, p. 409].

The scope has three limits. First, governance is seen from below. No one who set the rules was interviewed. Thus, what the study can say about the rule-making side is what reached the participants, including whatever came back in answer to their requests. Second, the rules in question govern the use of GenAI tools in analytical work, such as a chat assistant or an AI feature in an existing tool. They do not cover AI systems that an organisation develops or deploys, which are the subject of the lifecycle models discussed in Section 2.1. Finally, the setting is BI work. The study therefore makes no claim about knowledge work in general and, as case research, none about how common any practice is.

The study is about governance rather than adoption. Adoption research, including the work on BI system adoption, utilisation and success drawn on in Section 1.1 [@ain2019bisuccess], asks whether and why people take up a technology. In contrast, here the use is often already happening or wanted (Section 1.2). I argue that the open question is therefore how rules shape this use and what practitioners do in response.
