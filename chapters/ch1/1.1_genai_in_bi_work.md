## 1.1 Generative AI arrives in business intelligence work

Business intelligence (BI) sits between an organisation's data and its decisions. @ain2019bisuccess [p. 1] describe a BI system as "a suite of technological solutions that facilitates organizations to amass, integrate and analyse vast stocks of data in order to understand their opportunities, strengths and weaknesses". In practice, such a system is typically a combination of a data warehouse, online analytical processing and dashboards. It delivers two kinds of output. Reporting "involves the creation of reports through dragging and dropping", while analytics involves "business knowledge discovery and deep analysis with advanced statistical functions" [@ain2019bisuccess, p. 10]. In both cases, one person produces the output and another person acts on it. In this study, *BI work* therefore means producing, maintaining or supervising such analytical outputs, and the people who do this work are called BI practitioners (defined in Section 1.4).

However, research on BI has mostly looked at this work from the side of the organisation and the system. Reviewing 111 studies, @ain2019bisuccess [p. 8] find that organisational and system perspectives "were considered frequently while investigating the adoption, utilization or success of BI systems while the user perspective was less frequently considered". For example, the survey studies they review drew mostly on "managers, BI professionals and executives who were using BI systems" [@ain2019bisuccess, p. 4]. Yet the same authors place the return on BI investment in its use, which "in turn depends on the end-users" [@ain2019bisuccess, p. 10]. When users resist or underuse a BI system, the result can be "workflow problems, that ultimately produce strategy blindness and negative business performance" [@ain2019bisuccess, p. 10]. In other words, the value of BI is decided at the level of the person doing the work, and this is the level that the literature has studied least.

Generative AI (GenAI) is now changing what this work involves. For instance, assistants built on large language models can translate "natural language instructions into code" and return a finished analysis [@gu2024analystsverify, p. 1]. @gu2024analystsverify [p. 1] describe the consequence: "analysts' focus now shifts from writing and validating their own analyses to understanding and evaluating the analyses generated by AI assistants". This new task is not easy. Existing assistants offer little support for verification, and "even experts can be susceptible to automation bias and overreliance" [@gu2024analystsverify, p. 1]. Furthermore, such assistants can misread what the user intended or "make seemingly correct erroneous outputs" [@gu2024analystsverify, p. 1]. In their study of 22 professional analysts recruited from one "large data-driven software company" [@gu2024analystsverify, p. 8], participants "sometimes formed incorrect conclusions due to their reliance on the AI's procedure" [@gu2024analystsverify, p. 11]. In short, analysts no longer only write an analysis; they also have to check an analysis that they did not write.

This matters because of where the output goes. Since "conclusions from data analyses often inform high-impact decisions in science, business, and government" [@gu2024analystsverify, p. 1], an error that the analyst does not catch travels into a decision. Thus, BI work is a point where the risks of GenAI meet an organisation's concern for its data and for the decisions built on it. It is also the kind of work to which organisations' rules for GenAI use apply (Section 1.2). This study grew out of that work. I spent four to five years in BI-related roles across several industries, in organisations ranging from start-ups to large companies, and Section 3.5 discusses what this background brings to the interviews.
