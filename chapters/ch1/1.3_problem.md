## 1.3 The problem: governance seen only from above

The literature on organisational AI governance sees rules from where they are made. It defines governance as a system of rules, practices and processes that keeps conduct within acceptable boundaries [@mantymaki2022definingaigov, pp. 604–605], and it describes those rules as cascading from strategic to operational tiers, travelling through documents and training, and being in place before use begins (Section 2.1). Most of this is prescription rather than observation: the models state how rules should arrive, and few sources check how they do. What happens at the end of the cascade, where a rule meets a person doing the work, is rarely asked.

The people at that end are largely absent from the evidence. Of the 36 governance sources screened for this study, 28 or 29 contain no account of how staff below the rule-setting level receive AI governance (Section 2.6). Where practitioners have been studied, they were mostly people who had chosen to take on responsible AI work: the carriers of governance rather than its recipients. Where working around rules is documented, as in Section 1.2, it is documented through what executives say. None of the field studies takes BI work as its unit of analysis, although BI work is where the analytical outputs behind many decisions are produced.

The absence matters because a rule's effect is settled where it is received. Without the recipient's account, it cannot be known whether a rule shapes work as intended, is reinterpreted at the desk or is worked around, nor whether anything a practitioner learns travels back to those who set the rule. Governance seen only from above cannot answer these questions. This study takes them up from below (Section 1.4).
