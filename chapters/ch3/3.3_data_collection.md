## 3.3 Data collection

Data were collected through one semi-structured interview with each participant, designed to last 45 to 60 minutes, held on Microsoft Teams and conducted in English or Taiwanese Mandarin [PENDING: interviews] [FILL: interview period]. The sections ran in a fixed order: an opening, role and context, a critical incident (the core), how the rules arrived, how they work in practice, whether anything goes up, and a closing. Internal documents were discussed only in a separate, optional call of about ten minutes (Section 3.6).

The protocol was developed in the four phases of the Interview Protocol Refinement framework [@castillomontoya2016ipr, p. 812]. First, each research question was mapped to the interview sections that answer it. Second, the questions were written as plain conversation, avoiding judgemental terms such as "violation" or "mistake". Third, my supervisor read the design. Such feedback is meant to make a protocol more trustworthy [@castillomontoya2016ipr, p. 824]. He judged it long and suggested announcing the document option in advance. Finally, the protocol was piloted "with people who mirror the characteristics of the sample to be interviewed for the actual study" [@castillomontoya2016ipr, p. 827]. The pilot, in October 2026, used version 0.98 in Taiwanese Mandarin with a participant of the kind sampled in the breadth stratum. No fixed item had to be rephrased. However, it took up to about 65 minutes, mostly in the incident section. Version 1.0 therefore cut six questions, took one incident by default and moved the document walkthrough to the separate call. The pilot was not retained as data.

The core of each interview was a critical incident chosen by the participant. The technique "provides a rich source of data by allowing respondents to determine which incidents are the most relevant to them" [@gremler2004cit, p. 66]. However, "it is important to determine and report the criteria for whether an incident should be included in a study" [@gremler2004cit, p. 76]. Here, the criteria were a bounded menu of three types: a refused or restricted use, an output that could not be used as it was, and a use that got approved. Each incident was traced with four fixed questions (what happened, what rule existed, what changed afterwards, who decided), plus probes on whether a one-off decision had become a standing rule and what came back when something was passed up. The accounts are retrospective. The technique's originator acknowledged that retrospective self-report could be used [Flanagan, 1954, as cited in @butterfield2005cit, p. 480], and almost all the CIT studies that Butterfield et al. reviewed from 1987 onwards relied on it [-@butterfield2005cit, p. 481]. Accuracy, in his view, "can be deduced from the level of full, precise details given by the participant" [@butterfield2005cit, p. 478]. Therefore, the probes asked for concrete detail, and confidence in dates was recorded rather than pressed.

Of the later sections, Section C asked how the rules arrived. Participants who had used generative AI before the rules existed were also asked what they stopped, changed or kept doing, and later arrivals what they found in place. Section D asked about technical controls and about what happens to data before it goes into an AI tool, noting whether the answer was rule-shaped or case-shaped (Section 3.4). Section E asked whether a route upward existed and had been used and, where nothing went up, the participant's reason. Questions on change went only to those present before the rules, and tier-specific probes only when time allowed. The closing asked what had been missed, in depth cases whether colleagues would see it differently, and offered the summary check (Section 3.5).

The English version was the master, and I conducted the Mandarin interviews myself. The purpose statement, the incident menu, the four trace questions, the escalation and confidence probes, and the probes on use before the rules, on data before it enters an AI tool and on why nothing went up were fixed in both languages and read as written. Other probes were rendered live. The Mandarin wording was checked through the pilot only. It was not back-translated, and no second bilingual reader reviewed it. Translation of quotations is covered in Section 3.5.

Three further procedures surrounded the interviews. First, the document call recorded version numbers and dates only, nothing was copied, and quotation required a separate permission (Section 3.6). Second, Depth A interviews were scheduled close together, each participant was asked not to discuss the interview until all were done, and no one was told what another had said [PENDING: Depth A interviews]. Third, a contact summary followed within 24 hours (Section 3.4). In short, every participant met the same core questions in the same order, and the post-pilot changes shortened the interview without altering that core.
