This study applies the four criteria that @nowell2017trustworthiness [p. 3] carry into thematic analysis (credibility, transferability, dependability and confirmability) to the procedure described above, rather than asserting them in general. First, dependability and confirmability rest on an audit trail, which "provides readers with evidence of the decisions and choices made by the researcher" [@nowell2017trustworthiness, p. 3]. Here, the trail is a version-controlled repository holding the dated design documents, the protocol versions and the codebook with its change log, together with the contact summaries and memos kept on Aalto OneDrive [PENDING: change log, summaries and memos kept during fieldwork].

One researcher coded all the data, so no inter-rater reliability was calculated. In codebook thematic analysis, such measures are not usually treated as markers of quality [@braun2021onesize, p. 333]. Instead, two consistency checks were used. I re-coded [FILL: n] transcripts after an interval of [FILL: interval] and compared the two codings, and my supervisor reviewed a sample of [FILL: n] anonymised coded excerpts [PENDING: both checks]. Neither is a reliability measure. @fereday2006hybrid [p. 91], who used a similar single-coder arrangement, note that it "allowed for consistency in the method but failed to provide multiple perspectives". The same limit applies here.

Second, credibility was supported by offering each participant a short summary of what I had understood, so that misreadings could be corrected. [FILL: n] accepted and [FILL: n] returned corrections [PENDING: member checking]. Third, transferability concerns case-to-case transfer rather than generalisation to a population [@nowell2017trustworthiness, p. 3]. It therefore depends on describing each case well enough for a reader to judge fit. However, the anonymity commitments in Section 3.6 limit that description to sector, jurisdiction and tier, a cost accepted for the participants' protection.

As a native speaker of Taiwanese Mandarin, I conducted and translated these interviews myself. An AI tool could produce a first draft of a translation from the de-identified transcript, but I checked every quoted passage against the original and decided its final English wording (Section 3.6). Transcripts were coded in the language of the interview, and only passages quoted in the thesis were translated into English [PENDING: translation of quoted passages]. @temple2004translation [p. 164] hold that "there is no neutral position from which to translate", and that the researcher-translator role is "inextricably bound also to the socio-cultural positioning of the researcher" [-@temple2004translation, p. 168]. Therefore, the English wording of each quotation is my interpretation.

Participants were personal contacts, several of them former colleagues, and I share their background in BI and analytics work. Insider status can make participants "more open with researchers" [@dwyer2009insider, p. 58]. However, it also brings assumptions about how rules work in organisations like theirs. Corbin Dwyer and Buckle argue that researchers "can only ever occupy the space between" [-@dwyer2009insider, p. 61] and recommend "disciplined bracketing" [-@dwyer2009insider, p. 59]. My bracketing device was a memo written before each interview, recording what I already knew about the participant's generative AI use and their organisation's rules. Anything recorded there counted as data only if the participant raised it [PENDING: pre-interview memos].
