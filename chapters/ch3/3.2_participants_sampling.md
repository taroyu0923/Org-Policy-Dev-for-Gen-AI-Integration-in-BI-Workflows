The study defines BI practitioners as people who produce, maintain or directly supervise analytical outputs that others use to make decisions, and who neither set their organisation's rules for generative AI use nor hold a formal responsible-AI or AI-governance role. The first clause is the inclusion criterion. It admits data engineers, who maintain the pipelines that analytical outputs depend on, planning and business-operations roles whose analysis others act on, and first-line managers of such work. In contrast, it excludes general software and product roles with no analytical output. The second clause keeps rule owners outside the sample. The third separates it from @rakova2021practitionerperspectives [p. 7:7], whose interviewees were recruited because aspects of their work were "related to the field of responsible AI". Each participant's eligibility was checked against the criterion before recruitment.

The sample comprised 14 participants in nine organisations in five countries, three of them outside the EU (Table 3.1) [FILL: final n if anyone withdraws]. Most participants worked at the operational tier, and the most senior were at a senior tactical level. Thus, the sample contains no strategic tier and no one who authored a generative AI policy. Digital platforms dominate the sector mix, with manufacturing and financial services as contrasts. Six interviews were planned in Taiwanese Mandarin and eight in English.

This is a convenience sample drawn from my professional network, with purposive stratification by tier, sector and jurisdiction. It is not a sample drawn from a frame. Therefore, it is defended on the variation it offers in regulatory exposure and organisational type, not on representativeness. Each participant was invited individually, and employers were not approached. However, one confound follows from the network. In Depth B, EU jurisdiction coincides with engineering rather than analyst roles. The breadth stratum separates the two only partly, with two EU cases in analytical roles and two Taiwanese cases in analyst roles.

Sample size was set by information power rather than saturation. In other words, "the more information the sample holds, relevant for the actual study, the lower amount of participants is needed" [@malterud2016informationpower, p. 1753]. Of their five considerations, four favoured a modest sample here: a focused aim, participants chosen specifically for it, an established theoretical spine, and incident-anchored dialogue with interviewees who were professional peers. However, the fifth pulled the other way, since a larger sample is needed "if cross-case analysis is conducted" [-@malterud2016informationpower, p. 1756]. The planned number was therefore an initial approximation whose adequacy "must be evaluated continuously during the research process" [-@malterud2016informationpower, p. 1759] [PENDING: adequacy appraised during fieldwork].

Finally, four participants, in Depth B and the breadth stratum, were expected to have joined their organisations after generative AI rules were already in place [PENDING: confirmed in interviews (A3, C1)]. They were asked about what they found on arrival and how they learned it, rather than about how the rules had changed.
