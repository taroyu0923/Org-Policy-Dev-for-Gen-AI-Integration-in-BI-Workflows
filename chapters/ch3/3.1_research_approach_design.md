## 3.1 Research approach and design

This study is qualitative and interpretive. The research question asks how BI practitioners encounter, interpret and act on their organisation's rules for generative AI use, and its answer is a sequence. It starts with a use or attempted use, continues with a rule met on the way and what the practitioner did next, and ends with whether anything travelled upward. A survey would have recorded something else. For example, @silic2025shadowai [pp. 6–7] asked 140 respondents whether they had heard of a requirement to approve AI use, and their evidence of employees working around rules came from executives [-@silic2025shadowai, p. 9]. Such an instrument measures awareness across a population. However, it cannot follow one person from a blocked request to what they did next. The trade-off also runs the other way. Case research is "ill-equipped to address the questions 'how often,' and 'how many'" [@eisenhardt2007theorybuilding, p. 27], so this study makes no claim about how common any practice is. Each interview account was anchored in specific incidents that the participant chose from a bounded menu. Thus, each report was an episode of work rather than an opinion (Section 3.3) [PENDING: interviews Oct 5–19; menu confirmed after the pilot].

The design is an embedded multiple-case design, in the typology of single, multiple, holistic and embedded designs that @baxter2008casestudy [pp. 549–550] set out from Yin's work. The case is the organisation, bounded by its rules for generative AI use as they are met in BI work. A boundary states "what will and will not be studied" [@baxter2008casestudy, p. 547], and this one excludes AI use outside analytical work. The units of analysis are practitioner accounts within each case. The design has two strata. First, the depth stratum comprises Depth A, the Taiwan office of a large multinational digital platform company (four participants: operational, tactical and senior tactical tiers), and Depth B, a mid-sized software company in Finland (three participants: operational and tactical). Second, the breadth stratum comprises Breadth 1 to 7, one participant each [FILL: final n per case after fieldwork]. A single-informant breadth case has one embedded unit and functions in practice as a holistic case. Therefore, "embedded" applies fully to the depth stratum only.

The two strata answer different questions. Multiple cases show "whether an emergent finding is simply idiosyncratic to a single case or consistently replicated by several cases" [@eisenhardt2007theorybuilding, p. 27]. The depth cases compare tiers within one rule system, while the breadth cases add variation in sector and jurisdiction. Using Baxter and Jack's distinction between analysis within, between and across subunits [-@baxter2008casestudy, p. 550], the strata were analysed separately and never pooled, since pooling would weight one organisation four times [PENDING: analysis after fieldwork]. However, the cases were not theoretically sampled in Eisenhardt and Graebner's sense [-@eisenhardt2007theorybuilding, p. 27]. I recruited them through my professional network (Section 3.2), so the design supports comparison rather than replication logic. Finally, the design sees governance from below, because no one who set the rules was interviewed.
