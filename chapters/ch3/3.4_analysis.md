The interview data were analysed with codebook thematic analysis [PENDING: coding after fieldwork]. Braun and Clarke distinguish three families of thematic analysis. Codebook approaches "use some kind of structured coding framework for developing and documenting the analysis" and sit within a qualitative paradigm. Moreover, they do not usually treat "consensus between coders and inter-rater reliability" as measures of quality [@braun2021onesize, p. 333]. That label describes this study's procedure, which used a codebook written before the first interview, with definitions and inclusion and exclusion rules, and a theoretical frame chosen in advance. In contrast, reflexive thematic analysis involves "no use of any coding framework" [@braun2021onesize, p. 334], so claiming it would misdescribe what was done. One researcher coded all transcripts [PENDING: coding]. Re-coding after an interval and a supervisor's review of a sample were consistency checks, not reliability measures (Section 3.5) [PENDING: both checks].

The codebook combined codes set in advance with codes added during coding. This is the hybrid approach in which @fereday2006hybrid [pp. 82–83] pair a deductive template of codes with data-driven inductive coding. As in their procedure, each code carried a label, a definition and a description of "how to know when the theme occurs" [@fereday2006hybrid, p. 85], which the codebook expresses as include and exclude rules. The a priori spine was the structural, procedural and relational typology of governance practices [@tallon2013informationartifact, p. 142; @responsibleaigovreview, p. 10], defined in Section 2.6. In the codebook, it is worded as a practitioner meets each practice: who holds decision rights, how rules operate in documents and tools, and how they move between people. The three interview phases served as secondary seed codes. Sensemaking, the second lens in Section 2.6, served as a sensitising concept, the term used by @balasooriya2026sensemaking [p. 7923]. Unlike in their study, however, it added no codes or themes. Instead, it guided the memos: which cues prompted an interpretation, which reading the participant judged plausible [@weick2005organizing, p. 415], and with whom it was talked over [PENDING: memos written during fieldwork]. Everything specific to BI work was left to inductive codes [PENDING: inductive codes added during coding; see codebook change log]. In other words, the spine was a starting frame, not the themes. Before coding, I fixed a rule that no a priori code could be the answer to the research question. The reasons are that themes "must be analytic outputs, not inputs" [@braun2021onesize, p. 342], and that reporting the sections of an interview schedule as themes is a recognised pitfall [@braun2006thematic, p. 94].

Two sets of attributes were recorded alongside the codes [PENDING: attributes recorded per participant and incident]. First, case attributes, one value per participant, were tier, sector, jurisdiction, interview language, whether the participant joined before or after their organisation's rules existed, and whether documents were available. Second, incident attributes, one set per incident, included the source of the working rule (document, ruling, colleague, tool block or own judgement) and the branch taken when a case was unclear (ask, act now, or consult peers only). They also included how far anything travelled upward, what came back, and, where nothing went up, the participant's own reason. Furthermore, the loop that structures Chapter 2 ordered the memos. It runs from an unclear case to a fork, a ruling or own judgement, a working rule, and whether anything went up. It served as a sensitising lens rather than a set of codes, and departures from it were recorded as candidate findings [PENDING: memo analysis].

Before each interview, I wrote down what I already knew about the participant's generative AI use and their organisation's rules. Such knowledge therefore counted as data only if the participant raised it (Section 3.5) [PENDING: pre-interview memos]. A contact summary followed within 24 hours and a familiarisation memo within 48 hours, familiarisation being the first phase in @braun2006thematic [p. 87]. Each memo also recorded whether the account confirmed, contradicted or did not address the expectations set out in Chapter 2. This keeps the deductive side of the analysis visible rather than implicit [PENDING: contact summaries and memos written during fieldwork]. Transcripts were coded in [FILL: coding software], and every change to the codebook was logged with its date and reason [PENDING: codebook change log]. Analysis ran within each case first and then across cases, with the depth and breadth strata kept separate (Section 3.1). Finally, a coverage matrix set organisations against the three phases [PENDING: cross-case analysis].

For Depth A, a threshold for reporting change over time was fixed on 29 September 2026, before any interview in that case. It was met if at least three of the four participants independently described the same change in the organisation's rules, with a rough date and a named trigger. In that case, evolution would be reported as a findings section. Two of four, or agreement on the change but not on its date or trigger, would be reported as a partial, contested account. Fewer than two would mean reporting what practitioners could and could not see of change. In short, the rule was fixed in advance so that the strand could not be shaped by what the interviews produced [PENDING: threshold outcome after Depth A interviews].
