### 1. WHY — Research Problem and Motivation
The **critical incident technique (CIT)** has evolved over 50 years from a task-analysis tool using direct observation into a qualitative research method relying heavily on **retrospective self-reports** of psychological constructs [1-4]. This evolution led to inconsistent terminology, divergent data-analysis procedures, and a lack of standardized reliability and validity measures [2, 5, 6]. The purpose of this article is to consolidate knowledge about the CIT, analyze its place within qualitative research, examine 19 graduate studies at the University of British Columbia (UBC), and propose a **standardized protocol of nine credibility checks** while preserving the method's inherent flexibility [1, 7-10].
*(Location: Abstract, p. 475; Introduction, pp. 475–477)* [1, 2, 7]

---

### 2. HOW — Method / Design
* **Type of Study**: Qualitative literature review, methodological synthesis, and framework design [1, 7, 11].
* **Data / Corpus Size**: Reviewed **over 125 publications** (74 journal articles, 9 books, 44 dissertations and theses, 3 paper presentations, and 1 report) [11], alongside a targeted analysis of **19 master's theses and doctoral dissertations** conducted at UBC between 1991 and 2003 [8].
* **Databases / Time Span**: Literature spanning from **1949 to 2003** (covering 50 years of CIT usage following Flanagan's 1954 landmark paper) [1, 11].
*(Location: Origin, description, and evolution of the CIT, p. 477; Emerging credibility/trustworthiness checks, pp. 488–489)* [8, 11]

---

### 3. WHAT — Key Findings and Frameworks Proposed
* **Four Major Historical Departures**: Identified how CIT shifted from Flanagan's original design via (1) studying psychological states/constructs rather than overt behaviors, (2) relying on retrospective self-reports rather than direct observation, (3) using diverse data-analysis approaches, and (4) applying varied credibility measures [3-6, 12]. *(Location: Evolution of the CIT, pp. 481–484)* [3-6, 12]
* **Paradigm Versatility**: Positioned CIT across both **positivist and post-modern/interpretive qualitative paradigms**, formalizing its features within Creswell's qualitative traditions framework [13-15]. *(Location: The CIT and other qualitative methods, pp. 484–486)* [13-15]
* **Framework of Nine Credibility Checks**: Proposed a standardized 9-step credibility protocol for CIT studies: (1) independent extraction of incidents (25%), (2) participant cross-checking, (3) independent judges placing incidents into categories (25%), (4) tracking exhaustiveness/saturation, (5) expert opinion, (6) participation rate (≥25% rule), (7) theoretical agreement, (8) descriptive validity (recording/transcription), and (9) interview fidelity [9, 10, 16-24]. *(Location: Emerging credibility/trustworthiness checks, pp. 488–492; Future directions and recommendations, pp. 493–494)* [9, 10]
