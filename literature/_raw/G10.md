### 1. WHY — Research Problem and Motivation
* **Research Problem:** Existing strategy literature on sensemaking and sensegiving predominantly adopts a macro-process view centered on top executives' conscious cognitive schemes and self-referential narratives, leaving an "asocial" gap regarding how frontline middle managers utilize tacit knowledge and socio-cultural structures during daily interactions [1-3].
* **Motivation:** Strategic change execution hinges on micro-level daily practices at the organizational interface; understanding how middle managers interpret and sell change through mundane routines is essential to explaining how sustainable competitive advantage is produced in practice [4-7].
* **Locations:** Section **"INTRODUCTION"** (pp. 1413–1414); Section **"STRATEGIC SENSEMAKING AND SENSEGIVING"** (pp. 1414–1416); Section **"CONCLUSION"** (pp. 1437–1438).

---

### 2. HOW — Method and Design
* **Study Type:** Exploratory qualitative single-case study of an ethnographic type [8].
* **Data & Data Collection:** Triangulated qualitative data design comprising semi-participant observation (4 days per week over 6 months), semi-structured interviews, and document analysis [8].
* **Sample & Corpus Size:** Single Canadian top-of-the-line women's clothing company (~\$6 million CAN sales, ~200 employees, 15 management team members); focused on 2 key middle managers (sales manager and collection manager) across 39 analyzed action units reconstituted from representative routines and conversations [9-12].
* **Databases / Source Material:** Primary observational field notes, publicity press kits, promotional show materials, and transcribed operational conversations [8, 11, 13].
* **Time Span:** 6-month field immersion during the early implementation phase of strategic change (preparation and presentation of a new collection) [8, 11].
* **Locations:** Section **"RESEARCH DESIGN"** (pp. 1417–1419); Section **"DESCRIPTION OF ROUTINES AND CONVERSATIONS"** (p. 1419); Section **"ANALYSIS OF ROUTINES AND CONVERSATIONS"** (p. 1424).

---

### 3. WHAT — Key Findings, Frameworks, and Typologies
* **Typology of Four Micro-Practices:**
  1. **Translating the new orientation:** Selecting strategic elements and connecting them via material and discursive symbols (e.g., fashion rhetoric) tailored to the counterpart's linguistic register [14-16].
  2. **Overcoding the strategy:** Inscribing strategic discourse into professional and socio-cultural codes (cultural/linguistic backgrounds, gender dynamics) to reinforce legitimacy [17-19].
  3. **Disciplining the client:** Organizing space, body language, and emotional displays (inciting desire, masking negative feelings) to subtly enlist external stakeholders [20-22].
  4. **Justifying the change:** Leveraging client-centered discourse ("what clients want") to legitimize market repositioning while deflecting attention from internal firm problems [23-25].
* **Framework of Explanation (Third-Order Explanation):**
  * Proposes a **third-order explanation** that extends beyond top management's structural plans (1st order) and strategic narrative patterns (2nd order) by highlighting co-present, mundane micro-practices embedded in tacit knowledge and social contexts [74, 75, 76; Table V, p. 1433].
* **Locations:** Section **"ANALYSIS OF ROUTINES AND CONVERSATIONS"** (pp. 1424–1431); Section **"STRATEGIC SENSEMAKING AND SENSEGIVING IN PRACTICE"** (pp. 1431–1433, Table V, p. 1433).

### §13 THESIS ANCHOR

#### (a) Verbatim Definitions of Focal Concepts

* **Reciprocal Relationship**: *"...sensemaking and sensegiving are less distinct domains (Hopkinson, 2001) than two sides of the same coin – one implies the other and cannot exist without it."* (*Strategic Sensemaking and Sensegiving*, p. 1414) [19]
* **Third-Order Explanation**: *"A third-order explanation of strategic sensemaking and sensegiving... explores how strategic sensemaking and sensegiving processes are constituted through communication and action in daily routines and conversations."* (*Strategic Sensemaking and Sensegiving in Practice*, p. 1431) [16]

  * *Translating the New Orientation*: *"Translating involves telling people the stories they want to hear and comprises two superposed actions (Hopkinson, 2001): (1) the elements used to translate the message must be chosen (selecting); (2) the material and discursive symbols that belong to the language of the other person must be used to transmit the message (connecting)."* (*Translating the New Orientation*, pp. 1423–1424) [4, 33]
  * *Overcoding the Strategy*: *"The act of inscribing words and actions around the strategy in the appropriate professional and socio-cultural codes of the interlocutor is called overcoding (Sfez, 1984)."* (*Overcoding the Strategy*, p. 1425) [7]
  * *Disciplining the Client / Other*: *"Disciplining the clients means that middle managers, through their routines and conversations, produced subjective and emotional effects whose objective is insidiously to 'sell' the new strategic orientation to the client..."* (*Disciplining the Client*, p. 1427) [10]
  * *Justifying the Change*: *"Providing 'good' reasons for the outside actor to adopt the change corresponds to the micropractice of justifying."* (*Justifying the Change*, p. 1428) [13]

#### (b) Main Claims and Propositions
* **Main Claim [Verbatim Quote]**: *"Strategic sensemaking and sensegiving processes are the result of a complex set of mundane micro-practices embedded in tacit knowledge and social contexts."* (*Strategic Sensemaking and Sensegiving in Practice*, p. 1432) [34]
* **Main Claim [Verbatim Quote]**: *"By focusing on tacit knowledge rather than examining middle managers’ explicit perceptions of change and their roles in it, this analysis reveals the underlying micropractices inherent to the skill accomplishment of knowing agents..."* (*Enriching the Understanding of Middle Managers’ Roles*, p. 1434) [35]
* **Main Proposition [Close Paraphrase]**: Strategic change is an ongoing, chronic feature of organizational life, and invisible, mundane daily routines and conversations matter just as much as formally orchestrated events or official strategy documents in producing strategic outcomes and sustaining competitive advantage (*Strategic Sensemaking and Sensegiving in Practice*, p. 1432; *Conclusion*, p. 1436) [17, 34, 36].
* **Main Proposition [Close Paraphrase]**: Middle managers act as pivotal strategic intermediaries at the organizational interface, using semantic and socio-cultural tacit knowledge to interpret (translate/overcode) and sell (discipline/justify) strategic change to external stakeholders (*Introduction*, pp. 1413–1414; *Enriching the Understanding of Middle Managers’ Roles*, pp. 1433–1435) [22, 26, 37-39].
* **Link to Issue Selling [Close Paraphrase]**: The micro-practices of "disciplining the client" and "justifying the change" operationalize and extend issue-selling mechanisms by employing impression management, emotional displays, and client-centered rhetoric to secure stakeholder buy-in (*Enriching the Understanding of Middle Managers’ Roles*, p. 1434) [21, 22].

#### (c) Mechanisms, Conditions, and Antecedents
* **Key Mechanisms**:
  1. *Semantic Tacit Knowledge*: Allows managers to convey message cores through implicit meanings and shared industry rhetoric without requiring formal definitions for every term (*Strategic Sensemaking and Sensegiving*, p. 1415) [3].
  2. *Socio-Cultural Tacit Knowledge & "Doing Gender"*: Mobilizing shared background structures (language, ethnicity, gender codes, social conventions) as symbolic resources to establish rapport and legitimize strategic change (*Strategic Sensemaking and Sensegiving*, p. 1415; *Overcoding the Strategy*, pp. 1425–1427) [13, 16, 51–55].
