### 1. WHY — Research Problem and Motivation
While strategy literature well documents that intended strategies often lead to unintended outcomes, rationalist models fail to explain how this divergence occurs during implementation [1]. The paper addresses the research gap regarding how middle managers—acting as change recipients—interpersonally interpret top-down mandates through lateral, informal interactions, turning planned interventions into emergent, unpredictable realities [1, 2].
* **Location:** Section "Abstract" (p. 1573) and Section "Introduction" (pp. 1573–1574) [1, 2].

---

### 2. HOW — Method and Design
* **Type of Study:** Real-time, longitudinal, qualitative, embedded single-case study grounded in an interpretive, processual approach [3].
* **Context / Sample:** "Anonco" (pseudonym), a recently privatized UK utility company undergoing radical restructuring into three new divisions (Core, Maintenance, Services) [3, 4].
* **Data & Corpus Size:** Qualitative monthly diaries kept by **26 middle-manager diarists**, supplemented by transcribed individual/group interviews, observational field notes of workshops and meetings, and official corporate documents (change plans, team briefs, videos) [3, 5].
* **Databases:** None (primary qualitative field data) [3, 5].
* **Time Span:** 16 months of real-time tracking during the transition phase (March 1993 to July 1994), analyzed across three distinct sensemaking periods (T1: April–Sept 1993; T2: Oct 1993–March 1994; T3: April–July 1994) [3, 4, 6, 7].
* **Location:** Section "Research Study: Methodology and Data Collection" (pp. 1576–1578), Section "The Case Study" (p. 1578), Section "Data Analysis" (pp. 1578–1579), and Section "Second-Order Findings" (p. 1589) [3-6].

### 3. WHAT — Key Findings, Frameworks, and Typologies
* **Cyclical Sensemaking Model of Emergent Change:** Demonstrates that strategy implementation is an ongoing, cyclical process where early sensemaking triggers and outcomes become inputs for subsequent sensemaking cycles [6, 8].
  * *Location:* Section "Second-Order Findings" (pp. 1587–1589) [6, 8].
* **Typology of Social Interaction Processes (Vertical vs. Lateral):** Distinguishes formal vertical interactions (senior-to-middle management) from informal lateral interactions (peer-to-peer gossip, stories, rumors), showing that lateral processes drive the vast majority of recipient sensemaking [9].
  * *Location:* Section "Discussion and Conclusion" (p. 1595) [9].
* **Typology of Emergent Outcomes (Congruent vs. Counteracting):** Categorizes implementation outcomes into *congruent consequences* (aligned with leadership intent) and *counteracting consequences* (unintended outcomes blocking change, such as turf protection and "business as usual" inertia) [5, 10-12].
  * *Location:* Section "Data Analysis" (p. 1579) & Section "First-Level Findings" (pp. 1581–1585) [5, 10, 11].
* **Role of Design Flaws as Sensemaking Triggers:** Reveals that structural omissions and ambiguous role boundaries ("black holes") trigger defensive lateral interactions that reinforce legacy mental schemata [8, 11, 13].
  * *Location:* Section "First-Level Findings" (p. 1583) & Section "Second-Order Findings" (pp. 1587, 1592) [8, 11, 13].

### §13 THESIS ANCHOR

**(a) Verbatim Definitions of the Focal Concepts:**
*   **Sensemaking**: `"Sensemaking is primarily a conversational and narrative process (Brown 2000; Gephart 1993, 1997) involving a variety of communication genre (Watson and Bargiela-Chiappini 1998), both spoken and written, and formal and informal."` [1] (Section: Change, Cognition and Sensemaking, p. 1576).
*   **Sensemaking Mode**: `"...sensemaking involves 'conversational and social practices' (Gephart 1993: 1469)."` [1] (Section: Change, Cognition and Sensemaking, p. 1576). Furthermore, intersubjective sensemaking is defined where `"Individual representations (thoughts, feelings, "intentions") become merged or synthesized ... not directly into supra-interactive structures, but into face-to-face conversations and inter-actions" (Wiley 1988: 258)"` [1, 8] (Section: Change, Cognition and Sensemaking, p. 1575–1576).
*   **Schemata**: `"Schemata are the mental models held by individuals that affect the events individuals respond to and how (Schutz 1964; Fiske and Taylor 1991; Labianca et al. 2000). *   **Social Processes of Interaction**: `"Social processes of interaction are the conversational and social practices identified from the first-order analysis that the middle managers engaged in as they attempted to make sense of the new structure and their new roles and responsibilities."` [6] (Section: Second-Order Findings: Linking Change Recipient Sensemaking to Emergent Change Outcomes, p. 1587).
*   **Developing Schemata**: `"Developing schemata are the interpretations that change recipients arrive at, through their social processes of interaction, of what change is about, such as the negative interpretations of the new structure."` [6] (Section: Second-Order Findings: Linking Change Recipient Sensemaking to Emergent Change Outcomes, p. 1587).

**(b) Main Claims and Propositions:**
*   **Lateral Sensemaking Primacy [Quoted]**: `"Through this framework we are able to build on what we already know about cognition and change to show that the interpretations middle managers arrive at concerning how they should respond to the change interventions arise primarily from lateral, informal social processes of interaction between themselves."` [21] (Section: Introduction, p. 1574).
*   **Role of Informal Peer Interaction [Quoted]**: `"As they seek to put the change plans of their seniors into action, their everyday experiences of the actions and behaviours of others, and the stories, gossip, jokes, conversations and discussions they share with their peers about these experiences, shape their interpretations of what they should be doing."` [21] (Section: Introduction, p. 1574).
*   **Creation of Congruent vs. Counteracting Outcomes [Quoted]**: `"If the meanings and interpretations individuals develop are consistent with those intended by the instigators of the changes, they are likely to behave in a way consistent with intended outcomes, leading to congruent change consequences. If the meanings and interpretations developed by the recipients differ from those intended, the result may be counteracting change consequences."` [6] (Section: Second-Order Findings: Linking Change Recipient Sensemaking to Emergent Change Outcomes, p. 1587).

*   **Triggers & Antecedents**:
