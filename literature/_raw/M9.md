### Analysis of "Qualitative research and translation dilemmas"

---

### 1. WHY — Research Problem and Motivation
In cross-language qualitative research, translation is frequently treated as a routine, unacknowledged technical task or hidden procedure to establish semantic equivalence, ignoring its underlying epistemological, social, and political implications [1-4]. The authors argue that language actively constructs social reality rather than merely expressing it; therefore, translation is an active process of knowledge production that directly impacts representation and power [5-7]. Consequently, how researchers acknowledge, execute, and position translation must be brought into mainstream social science research methodology [1, 8].
* **Location**: "Abstract" (p. 161), "Introduction" (pp. 161–163), "EPISTEMOLOGICAL POSITION OF THE RESEARCHER" (pp. 163–165), and "Discussion" (pp. 175–176).

---

### 2. HOW — Method / Design
* **Type of Study**: Conceptual, theoretical, and methodological debate paper (a critical narrative review and epistemological critique) [1, 2, 7].
* **Data / Corpus / Examples Analyzed**: Synthesizes literature across qualitative methodology, translation studies, sociolinguistics, and Deaf studies/British Sign Language (BSL) [2, 5, 9-11]. It illustrates its conceptual arguments using published qualitative studies (e.g., Khanum 2001, Edwards 1998, Riessman 2000, Ladd 2003, Harris 1995) and the authors' personal/professional research experiences in multilingual and BSL settings [4, 12-16].
* **Sample/Corpus Size, Databases, & Time Span**: Not applicable. The paper is a conceptual methodological inquiry and does not draw on an empirical dataset, systematic database search, or bounded sample/time period [2, 7].
* **Location**: "Introduction" (pp. 161–163), "THE RESEARCHER AS TRANSLATOR" (pp. 169–172), "TRANSLATORS WORKING WITH RESEARCHERS" (pp. 172–175), and "Discussion" (pp. 175–176).

---

### 3. WHAT — Key Findings, Frameworks, or Typologies
* **Translation as Knowledge Production**: Translation is an interpretive, social-constructionist act that creates meaning rather than neutrally transferring objective facts across languages ("EPISTEMOLOGICAL POSITION OF THE RESEARCHER", pp. 163–165) [5, 6].
* **Invisibility and Power Hierarchies**: Hiding the translation act or adopting English as an unexamined baseline reinforces language oppression and masks power differentials between dominant and minority languages ("THE INFLUENCE OF CONDITIONS PERTAINING TO A LANGUAGE", pp. 165–168) [17, 18].
* **Modality Mismatch (BSL Example)**: Translating across visual/gestural modalities foregrounds the structural non-fit of languages and exposes how standardized qualitative data tools (e.g., NUD*IST) force text "freezing" ("THE INFLUENCE OF CONDITIONS PERTAINING TO A LANGUAGE", pp. 165–168) [11, 19, 20].
* **Positionality of the Researcher-as-Translator**: Combining the roles of researcher and translator provides reflexivity regarding meaning equivalence, but does not guarantee objective "truth" or bypass the researcher's socio-cultural positioning ("THE RESEARCHER AS TRANSLATOR", pp. 169–172) [14, 21, 22].
* **Translators as Key Informants**: Treating translators as reflexive key informants and co-researchers—engaging with their intellectual autobiographies—enhances construct validity by uncovering cultural assumptions ("TRANSLATORS WORKING WITH RESEARCHERS", pp. 172–175) [16, 23-25].

### 4. DEFINITION — Core Constructs
* **Translation Dilemmas**: Framed as the key methodological and epistemological choices provoked when qualitative research involves multiple languages:
  > *"specifically it explores three questions: whether methodologically it matters if the act of translation is identified or not; the epistemological implications of who does translation; and the consequences for the final product of how far the researcher chooses to involve a translator in research"* [1].

### §13 THESIS ANCHOR

**Focus:** The researcher-as-translator position; whether and how translation should be reported; the epistemological claims the paper makes about translation.

#### (a) The Source's Own Definition(s) of the Focal Concept
* **Translation in Qualitative Research:** Defined as qualitative research studies "where data are collected in more than one language and the research process, at whatever stage(s), involves acts of translation between languages" (Section "Introduction", p. 161 [15]).
* **Social Constructionist / Interpretive Epistemology of Translation:** Defined as an approach acknowledging "that your location within the social world influences the way in which you see it. If you subscribe to this view of social reality, then translators must also form part of the process of knowledge production" (Section "EPISTEMOLOGICAL POSITION OF THE RESEARCHER", p. 163 [1]).
* **Translation as Value Reconstruction:** Quoting Sherry Simon (1996) with approval: "The solutions to many of the translator’s dilemmas are not to be found in dictionaries, but rather in an understanding of the way language is tied to local realities, to literary forms and to changing identities. Translators must constantly make decisions about the cultural meanings which language carries, and evaluate the degree to which the two different worlds they inhibit are ‘the same’. These are not technical difficulties, they are not the domain of specialists in obscure or quaint vocabularies. . . . In fact the process of meaning transfer has less to do with finding the cultural inscription of a term than in reconstructing its value." (Section "EPISTEMOLOGICAL POSITION OF THE RESEARCHER", pp. 163–164 [2]).
* **Conceptual Equivalence:** Quoting Herbert Phillips (1960): Defined as "in absolute terms an insolvable problem" since "almost any utterance in any language carries with it a set of assumptions, feelings, and values that the speaker may or may not be aware of but that the field worker, as an outsider, usually is not" (Section "EPISTEMOLOGICAL POSITION OF THE RESEARCHER", p. 164 [16]).

#### (b) Its Main Claims or Propositions About the Focus
1. **Translation is an Active Process of Knowledge Production:**
   * *Verbatim Quote:* "centring translation and how it is dealt with raises issues of representation that should be of concern to all researchers" (Section "Abstract", p. 161 [17]; Section "Discussion", p. 174 [18]).
   * *Closely Paraphrased:* Translation is never a neutral, purely technical execution; from a social constructionist perspective, translators actively participate in producing knowledge, as there is no neutral vantage point from which to translate [1, 8, 19].
2. **Hiding Translation Writes Out the Terms of Knowledge Production:**
   * *Verbatim Quote:* "The final text in English writes out the terms on which it was produced." (Section "EPISTEMOLOGICAL POSITION OF THE RESEARCHER", p. 163 [1]).
   * *Closely Paraphrased:* Failing to identify and discuss translation implicitly adopts an unreflexive positivist stance that treats translation as an irrelevant technical detail solved behind the scenes, masking language hierarchies and power relations [1, 20-22].
3. **The Researcher-as-Translator Role Requires Reflexive Examination:**
   * *Verbatim Quote:* "The researcher/translator role offers the researcher significant opportunities for close attention to cross cultural meanings and interpretations and potentially brings the researcher up close to the problems of meaning equivalence within the research process. But as we have illustrated, this researcher/translator role is inextricably bound also to the socio-cultural positioning of the researcher..." (Section "THE RESEARCHER AS TRANSLATOR", p. 168 [23]).
   * *Closely Paraphrased:* Performing the dual role of researcher and translator does not guarantee objective truth or unmediated authenticity; rather, the researcher's social location and insider/outsider positioning continuously shape the validity and interpretation of the data [23-26].

#### (c) Mechanisms, Conditions or Antecedents
1. **Researcher Epistemological Stance:** Adopting an objectivist paradigm leads researchers to treat translation as a technical obstacle to be eliminated via standardized techniques (e.g., back translation) and hidden in reports; adopting a social constructionist stance makes researchers treat translation as an active, reportable interpretive choice [1, 27].
2. **Language Power and Structural Hierarchies:** Translating between dominant languages (e.g., English) and non-dominant or oppressed languages/modalities (e.g., BSL) creates societal pressure for "methodological expediency" and "domestication", making translation invisible unless consciously resisted through textual strategies [7, 22, 28-30].

4. **Funding and Resource Constraints:** Restricted budgets and short project timelines pressure researchers to isolate translators into brief, technical translation roles rather than involving them as equal collaborators throughout analysis [5, 32, 33].
5. **Translator Involvement as Key Informants:** Involving translators early to discuss their intellectual biographies and conceptual choices makes non-equivalence and cultural reasoning visible [3-5, 34].
