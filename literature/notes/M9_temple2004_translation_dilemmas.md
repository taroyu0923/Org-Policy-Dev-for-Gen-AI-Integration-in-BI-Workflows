## 1. WHY
Translation in cross-language qualitative research is usually handled as a technical step, and the authors argue it raises issues of representation: "centring translation and how it is dealt with raises issues of representation that should be of concern to all researchers." *(Abstract, p.161)* The paper is "concerned with qualitative research studies where data are collected in more than one language and the research process, at whatever stage(s), involves acts of translation between languages." *(Introduction, p.161)*

## 2. HOW
Conceptual and methodological debate paper, not an empirical study. It draws on translation studies, sociolinguistics and qualitative methodology, on the authors' own projects (Polish communities in the UK; British Sign Language and Deaf studies) and on published studies. The authors say the questions they chose are not the only ones (see §9). No dataset, database or time span.

## 3. WHAT
Three questions the paper explores: "whether methodologically it matters if the act of translation is identified or not; the epistemological implications of who does translation; and the consequences for the final product of how far the researcher chooses to involve a translator in research." *(Abstract, p.161)* Then, by section: the epistemological position of the researcher (pp.162–165); the influence of conditions pertaining to a language (pp.165–168); the researcher as translator (p.168 ff.); translators working with researchers (pp.170–175); Discussion (pp.175 ff.). *(Section start pages taken from heading matches; the last two are approximate.)*

## 4. DEFINITION
- **Scope of "translation":** studies "where data are collected in more than one language and the research process, at whatever stage(s), involves acts of translation between languages." *(p.161)*
- **Researcher as translator (a description, not a formal definition):** "The situation where the researcher is fluent in the language of communities she is working with is rare. It offers opportunities in terms of research methods that are not open to other researchers in cross language research." *(THE RESEARCHER AS TRANSLATOR, p.168; NotebookLM cited pp.169–171)*
- **Social constructionist position:** "that your location within the social world influences the way in which you see it. If you subscribe to this view of social reality, then translators must also form part of the process of knowledge production." *(p.164; NotebookLM said p.163)*
- **Translation as value reconstruction (Simon, 1996, quoted with approval):** "In fact the process of meaning transfer has less to do with finding the cultural inscription of a term than in reconstructing its value." *(quoted from Simon, 1996: 137–8, on p.165)*

## 13. THESIS ANCHOR (role: Chapter 3 method anchor; focus: the researcher-as-translator position; whether and how to report translation; epistemological claims)
- **(a) Definitions:** see §4. Conceptual equivalence, quoting Phillips (1960): the position of conceptual equivalence across languages is "'in absolute terms an insolvable problem' since 'almost any utterance in any language carries with it a set of assumptions, feelings, and values that the speaker may or may not be aware of but that the field worker, as an outsider, usually is not' (p. 291)." *(p.165; NotebookLM presented this as one quotation and said p.164)*
- **(b) Main claims:**
  - Translation is part of knowledge production if one holds the social constructionist view (p.164, §4).
  - Hiding translation hides how the text was made: "The final text in English writes out the terms on which it was produced." *(p.164)*
  - The researcher-as-translator role gives access to meaning and equivalence but is tied to the researcher's socio-cultural position (p.168, §5).
  - "centring translation and how it is dealt with raises issues of representation that should be of concern to all researchers." *(Abstract, p.161)*
- **(c) Conditions named (paraphrase):** the researcher's epistemological stance (objectivist versus constructionist); language hierarchies between dominant and non-dominant languages; software and transcription conventions; funding and time limits; involving translators as informants. *(From NotebookLM's list; each was not tied line by line to a page and is not quoted.)*
- **(d) Evidence base:** conceptual review with examples from the authors' projects and published studies (§2).
- **(e) Limitations:** see §9.
- **(f) What a careful examiner would expect** — verbatim, printed pages:
  - "What language was the data collected in? At what stage were the interviews translated and transcribed?
