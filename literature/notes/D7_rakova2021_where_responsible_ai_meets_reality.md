## 1. WHY
A proliferation of abstract AI ethics principles exists, but a critical gap remains between theoretical guidelines and operational industry reality, leaving practitioners struggling to translate abstract accountability into daily corporate practices. Motivation: examine how organizational culture and structure impact responsible-AI initiatives and propose a framework for shifting from reactive practices to an aspirational future state. *Location: Sec.1 Introduction, pp.7:1–7:2; Abstract p.7:1; Sec.3 p.7:6.*

## 2. HOW
Qualitative empirical study — semi-structured interviews, contextual-design affinity diagramming, and an interactive design workshop. Sample: 26 semi-structured interviews across 19 organizations on 4 continents (US, UK, Australia, Denmark, Japan), plus a workshop with 13 groups of 4–6 individuals (~52–78 participants total). Practitioners across 8 functional roles (Product Management, Policy, Engineering, ML Research, Legal, AI Strategy, HR, Marketing/Sales). Data collected late 2019 through submission Oct 2020. *Location: Sec.3, pp.7:6–7:8; Table 1 & text pp.7:7–7:8; Sec.4.1 p.7:9; Sec.A.3 p.7:23.*

## 3. WHAT
- Typology of Four Organizational Questions: "When and how do we act?", "How do we measure success?", "What are the internal structures we rely on?", "How do we resolve tensions?" *(Sec.4.1, Table 2, pp.7:9–7:10)*
- Three-state practice continuum: Prevalent (reactive, uncompensated labor) → Emerging (proactive evaluation, metrics frameworks) → Aspirational Future (anticipatory frameworks, societal-impact metrics). *(Sec.4.1, Table 2, pp.7:9–7:10)*
- Internal–external dynamics model: AI governance as an "open system" where internal grassroots change agents ("tempered radicals") interact with external reputational/regulatory pressures. *(Sec.1, p.7:2; Sec.2.2, pp.7:4–7:5)*
- Typology of four systemic enablers: multi-level veto power, balancing internal/external pressures, robust multi-level communication channels, interdependent practice pipelines. *(Sec.1, p.7:3; Sec.5, pp.7:17–7:18)*

## 4. DEFINITION
"We intend this to refer not only to fairness-related projects but also more broadly projects related to the work on responsible AI and accountability of ML products and services given the high degree of overlap in goals, research, and people working on these topics." *(Sec.3, p.7:6)* Structurational construct (after Orlikowski): human agents, mediating technology, and organizational properties — "internal business strategies, control mechanisms, ideology, culture, division of labor and procedures, communication patterns, as well as outside pressures..." *(Sec.2.2.1, p.7:5)*

## 5. CITABLE
Source addresses ML/AI governance broadly, pre-dating the GenAI policy boom; covers both practitioner enactment and executive/organizational design.
- "In several cases, the formation of a full-time team to conduct responsible AI work was only catalyzed by the results from volunteer-led investigations of potential bias issues within models that were en route to deployment. The volunteers for these investigations went far beyond their existing role descriptions, sometimes risking their own career progression, to take on additional uncompensated labor to prevent negative outcomes for the company." *(Sec.4.2.1, p.7:11)*
- "As Orlikowski points out, whether designing, appropriating, modifying, or even resisting technology, human agents are influenced by the properties of their organizational context." *(Sec.1, p.7:2)*
- "In a few cases, proactive champions organizing grassroots actions and internal advocacy with leadership have made responsible AI a company-wide priority, which then sometimes made it easier for people to get resourcing for responsible AI initiatives and to establish proactive organization-wide processes." *(Sec.4.2.2, p.7:11)*

## 7. INTERVIEW VALUE
HIGH — empirical interview study (n=26 practitioners across 19 organizations); the closest published analogue to Albert's own design. Maps directly onto all three phases:
- **Policy Encounter & Interpretation:** three-tiered organizational readiness model (proactive/reactive/exploratory) (Sec.3); tension between top-down principles and practitioner ambiguity about applying them to concrete cases (Sec.4.1).
- **Implementation & Change Mgmt:** friction translating abstract fairness/ethics principles into concrete metrics/checklists usable day to day (Sec.4.2); "responsible AI champions" as informal change agents distinct from formal policy owners (Sec.4.3).
- **Organizational Adaptation Dynamics:** incentive misalignment — responsible-AI work not rewarded in performance reviews or product timelines (Sec.4.4); the "enablers" framework (organizational culture, incentive structures, cross-functional collaboration, external accountability pressure) is directly usable as an a priori coding frame.

## 9. LIMITATIONS
1. Sample skews toward large, resource-rich technology companies with dedicated responsible-AI functions — findings "may not generalize to smaller organizations or those without dedicated responsible AI teams or roles." *(Discussion)*
2. Interviewees were largely self-selected volunteers already engaged in responsible-AI work, likely biasing toward more mature/positive organizational accounts. *(Limitations)*
3. Cross-sectional snapshot, not longitudinal — cannot capture how enablers evolve over time. *(Limitations)*
4. Broad scope across many AI/ML application domains rather than a specific functional population such as BI/reporting analysts — studies "practitioners building and deploying AI/ML systems" generally, not a specific occupational tier.

## 11. WORKING-TIER RECEPTION
Rich, directly on-topic empirical data — the strongest working-tier source found in the S3 batch. (a) Policy reaches practitioners unevenly and often abstractly, requiring local reinterpretation into concrete workflow steps (Sec.4.1-4.2). (b) Gaps are central to the paper's thesis: principles-to-practice translation failure, absence of actionable metrics, and incentive structures that do not reward responsible-AI effort (Sec.4.2, 4.4). (c) Attitudes mixed — practitioners often personally motivated but organizationally constrained; frustration with performative/box-ticking responses from leadership described though not with a specific quoted term. (d) Channels include informal "champion" networks, cross-functional working groups, and (where present) formal escalation/review processes — informal channels argued more effective than formal ones (Sec.4.3). (e) When guidance is silent or contested, practitioners rely on personal judgment, informal peer consultation, or defer to product/timeline pressure over ethical considerations (Sec.4.4).
