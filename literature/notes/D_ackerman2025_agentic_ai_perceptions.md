## 1. WHY
Motivated by the rapid pace of AI systems gaining autonomy, making value-alignment a critical priority. Core research question: "how do organizations' perceptions of agentic AI influence their implementation of responsible AI practices and their subsequent Return on Investment (ROI) calculations, including considerations of workforce skills and capabilities?" Addresses a "crucial gap in understanding how practitioners implement and adapt responsible AI frameworks when facing the unique challenges of agentic AI."
*Location: Section 1, p.2; Section 1.1, p.3.*

## 2. HOW
Interpretive qualitative research. Data collection: concise anonymous survey via Microsoft Forms. Sample: 44 AI professionals (consultants, developers, business leaders, researchers) at large North American organizations (5,000+ employees or $1B+ revenue). Literature databases: Google Scholar, Consensus, arXiv, EBSCO (Section 2.4, p.4). Two-week online survey period (Section 4, p.16).

## 3. WHAT
- Interpretive synthesis framework: Responsible AI Dimensions (Y-axis) × Emergent Themes (X-axis) heatmap. *(Section 3.3, p.6, Fig.1)*
- Five-part thematic typology: (1) Autonomy, Control & Ethical Alignment; (2) Organizational Culture, Practices & Societal Impacts; (3) Strategic Importance of Responsible AI; (4) Knowledge Gaps in the Agentic AI Landscape; (5) Challenges in Adapting Responsible AI Frameworks. *(Section 1.1/3.3, p.6)*
- 86% consensus that RAI frameworks need enhancement for agentic AI complexities. *(Section 3.8, p.13, Fig.6)*
- Stakeholder Engagement Processes was the least-selected enhancement priority (9%). *(Section 3.8, pp.13–14, Fig.7)*
- Generative vs. agentic AI conceptual confusion creates layered knowledge gaps. *(Section 3.4, p.8, Fig.2)*
- Only 48% feel "well prepared for future advancements"; only 41% have "clear understanding of how agentic AI impacts RAI policies and governance." *(Section 3.8, p.15, Fig.8)*
- 57% see a strong link between RAI practices and financial performance, but ROI calculation for agentic AI projects remains a core challenge. *(Section 3.6, p.10, Fig.4)*

## 4. DEFINITION
- **Responsible AI:** "designing, developing, and deploying AI systems in ethically, transparently, and alignment with societal values. It encompasses principles such as fairness, accountability, transparency, privacy, and inclusivity, aiming to minimize bias and harm while fostering trust." *(Section 3.3, p.6, fn.3)*
- **Responsible AI Frameworks:** "guide the development, deployment, and use of AI systems to minimize potential harm (Dehghani et al., 2024)." *(Section 1.1, p.2)*

## 9. LIMITATIONS (self-stated)
- Sample selection bias: recruited via researcher's professional networks/LinkedIn — "means the research may not capture broader perspectives and lacks statistical generalizability." (Section 2.3, p.4)
- Corporate/official-policy filtering: "Participants may have given responses reflecting organizational policies rather than individual views." (p.4)
- Temporal/stage constraints: "The dynamic nature of AI and agentic AI's early stage may limit the insights long-term value"; two-week survey window. (p.4; Section 4, p.16)
- Unaccountable extraneous variables: existing data-protection/risk/privacy practices may have influenced responses unaccountably. (p.4)
- Inherent survey biases: misinterpretation, overreporting, social desirability. (Section 4, p.16)
- Construct-validity confusion: data revealed "frequent confusion between generative and agentic AI," meaning some agentic-AI answers likely reflect generative-AI experience instead. (Section 3.6, p.10; Section 4, p.16)
