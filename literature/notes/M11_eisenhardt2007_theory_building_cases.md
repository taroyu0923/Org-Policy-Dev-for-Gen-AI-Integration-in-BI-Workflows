## 1. WHY
The paper is written for authors of case-based theory-building research who meet predictable objections in review. NotebookLM's summary (a paraphrase, not a quote): challenges arise from misunderstandings about sampling, informant bias and the presentation of data, and the paper offers responses to them. *Location: opening section and "Justifying Theory Building", pp.25–26.*

## 2. HOW
Methodological commentary ("From the Editor"), not an empirical study. It draws on published exemplar case studies (for example Eisenhardt 1989a, Graebner & Eisenhardt 2004, Hargadon & Sutton 1997, Weick 1993) and on the authors' experience. No data, sample, database search or time span. *Pp.25–30; reference list pp.30–32.*

## 3. WHAT
Sections of the paper (headings as printed) and what each recommends, as NotebookLM summarised them and as checked against the sections below:
- **Justifying Theory Building** — show why the question suits theory building and why existing theory does not answer it *(pp.25–26)*.
- **Theoretical Sampling of Cases** — select cases for theoretical reasons, not to represent a population; multiple cases give stronger theory than a single case *(pp.26–27)*.
- **Dealing with Interview Data** — limit informant bias with numerous, knowledgeable informants from diverse perspectives, and by combining retrospective and real-time cases *(p.28)*.
- **Presenting Empirical Evidence** — build the paper around the theory, with each part of the theory supported by evidence, and use tables and visual aids *(pp.28–29)*.
- **Writing the Emergent Theory** — present propositions with their logic, be transparent about how theory was inducted, and use the term "grounded theory building" carefully *(pp.29–30)*.

## 4. DEFINITION
- **Theory building from cases:** "Building theory from case studies is a research strategy that involves using one or more cases to create theoretical constructs, propositions and/or midrange theory from case-based, empirical evidence (Eisenhardt, 1989b)." *(p.25)*
- **Case studies:** "Case studies are rich, empirical descriptions of particular instances of a phenomenon that are typically based on a variety of data sources (Yin, 1994)." *(p.25)*
- **Replication logic:** "Central to building theory from case studies is replication logic (Eisenhardt, 1989b). That is, each case serves as a distinct experiment that stands on its own as an analytic unit." *(p.25)*
- **Theoretical sampling:** "Theoretical sampling simply means that cases are selected because they are particularly suitable for illuminating and extending relationships and logic among constructs." *(p.27)*
Note: the in-text citations "Yin, 1994" and "Eisenhardt, 1989b" belong to the source; the reference list gives Yin's *Case study research: Design and methods* as the 2nd edition (1994). The paper does not define "governance".

## 5. CITABLE
The paper does not address organisations' AI policy, BI or employee adaptation. Its citable claims are about design:
- "Like a series of related laboratory experiments, multiple cases are discrete experiments that serve as replications, contrasts, and extensions to the emerging theory (Yin, 1994)." *(p.25)*
- "while single-case studies can richly describe the existence of a phenomenon (Siggelkow, 2007), multiple-case studies typically provide a stronger base for theory building (Yin, 1994)." *(p.27)*
- "Multiple cases enable comparisons that clarify whether an emergent finding is simply idiosyncratic to a single case or consistently replicated by several cases (Eisenhardt, 1991). Multiple cases also create more robust theory because the propositions are more deeply grounded in varied empirical evidence." *(p.27)*
- "Constructs and relationships are more precisely delineated because it is easier to determine accurate definitions and appropriate levels of construct abstraction from multiple cases." *(p.27)*
- "adding three cases to a single-case study is modest in terms of numbers, but offers four times the analytic power." *(p.27; the sentence is quoted from its middle, as NotebookLM gave it)*
- "the purpose of the research is to develop theory, not to test it, and so theoretical (not random or stratified) sampling is appropriate." *(p.27)*
- "retain only the relationships that are replicated across most or all of the cases. Since there are typically fewer of these relationships than there are details in a richly observed single case, the resulting theory is often more parsimonious (and also more robust and generalizable)." *(p.30)*

## 13. THESIS ANCHOR (role: Chapter 3 method anchor; focus: why multiple cases, replication logic, theoretical sampling, how to present evidence across cases)
**(a) Definitions:** see §4.

**(b) Main claims (verbatim):** see §5 for multiple cases, replication and sampling. On presenting evidence across cases:
- "The best way to address this challenge of 'better stories vs. better theories' is to develop a theory in sections or by distinct propositions in such a way that each is supported by empirical evidence. Thus, the overarching organizing frame of the paper is the theory, and each part of the theory is demonstrated by evidence from at least some of the cases." *(p.29)*
- "it is critical to invest in developing well-crafted tables, appendixes, and visual aids to demonstrate the theory's underlying empirical support and the anticipated richness of the case data, and to tie those tables clearly to the text." *(p.29)*
- "multiple cases are chosen for theoretical reasons such as replication, extension of theory, contrary replication, and elimination of alternative explanations (Yin, 1994)." *(p.27)*

**(c) Conditions named by the paper** (paraphrase of the sections above): a "how"/"why" question where existing theory is inadequate (pp.25–26); sampling for theoretical reasons (p.27); informants who are numerous, knowledgeable and diverse, and a mix of retrospective and real-time cases (p.28); evidence organised around propositions with supporting tables (p.29).

**(d) Evidence base:** none of its own; commentary on published case research and the authors' experience. See §2.

**(e) Limitations:** see §9.

**(f) What a careful examiner would expect (verbatim; the paper frames these as ways for authors to meet reader challenges, not as a checklist):**
- Justify the method: "researchers also must take the added step of justifying why the research question is better addressed by theory-building rather than theory-testing research." *(p.26)* Also "clarify why the research question is significant, and why there is no existing theory that offers a feasible answer." *(p.26)* and "demonstrate that the existing research either does not address the research question at all, or does so in a way that is inadequate or likely to be untrue." *(p.26)*
- Sample theoretically: "clarify that the purpose of the research is to develop theory, not to test it, and so theoretical (not random or stratified) sampling is appropriate." *(p.27)* The paper's sentence on reasons for sampling reads: "so too are cases sampled for theoretical reasons, such as revelation of an unusual phenomenon, replication of findings from other cases, contrary replication, elimination of alternative explanations, and elaboration of the emergent theory." *(p.27; NotebookLM's version dropped "so too are")*
- Limit informant bias: "A key approach is using numerous and highly knowledgeable informants who view the focal phenomena from diverse perspectives." *(p.28)*
- Report the induction: "preempt misunderstanding by engaging in systematic data collection and theory development processes that are reported with transparent description, particularly regarding how the theory was inducted from the data (e.g., description of cross-case comparison techniques)." *(p.30)*
- On terminology (the sentence begins "A straightforward approach for coping with the varied meanings of “qualitative research” is to"): "avoid the term. Rather, clarify the research strategy being used, and contrast it with other 'qualitative' approaches with differing epistemological assumptions. Specifically, when inducting theory from cases, be explicit about the theory-building goal and to liberally use footnotes that sharpen the distinctions among the multiple meanings of qualitative research." *(p.28)* (The paper's own wording of the second sentence reads awkwardly, "and to liberally use"; the quote keeps it as printed.) On "grounded theory building": "it is often helpful to deal with the multiple meanings of 'grounded theory building' by avoiding the term unless one is actually using the Glaser and Strauss (1967) approach." *(p.30)*
**Framing caveat.** NotebookLM organised its (f) answer under "MUST DO" and "MUST AVOID" headings. That framing is its own. The paper presents these as challenges and the responses to them. Its warning against narrative overload ("If the researcher relates the narrative of each case, then the theory is lost and the text balloons", p.29) is a description of a trade-off, not a rule.
