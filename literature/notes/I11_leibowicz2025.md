## 1. WHY
As visual digital experience dominates how people understand the world, AI advances make it easier to manipulate visual content, accelerating deception, defamation, and harassment risks (pp. 1–2). While multistakeholder collaboration is widely touted as the optimal governance mechanism, there is a critical lack of empirical qualitative research on how civil-society, industry, media, and policy actors actually collaborate to develop and implement safeguards (Introduction, pp. 1–2; Section 1.2, p. 3). Motivation: investigate what real-world collaborative efforts reveal about synthetic-media challenges, solutions, and implementation pathways (Section 2, p. 4).

## 2. HOW
Qualitative, empirical study: semi-structured in-depth interviews + autoethnographic review of real-world governance initiatives (Section 3, p. 4). **23 interviews** (60 min each): 13 PAI stakeholders (3 civil society, 7 industry, 3 media — purposive sample from case-study participants) plus 10 policymaker participants (snowball sample, 6 North America, 4 Europe) working on synthetic-media legislation/standards (Section 3.2, p. 5). Two case studies via autoethnographic review of Partnership on AI (PAI) initiatives since 2018: Case 1 (Facebook's Deepfake Detection Challenge governance) and Case 2 (PAI's Synthetic Media Framework) (Section 3.1.1, p. 5). Timespan covers pre-2022 preparation (2017–2022) through post-2022 rapid regulatory pressure triggered by ChatGPT (Section 4.4.3, p. 13).

## 3. WHAT
- Core threat is misrepresentation: "deceptive misrepresentation, impersonation, and the loss of control" over likeness/content origin, not the technology itself (pp. 7–8).
- Asymmetric dual-use timelines: malicious uses materialize faster and need less technical sophistication than beneficial ones (pp. 8–9).
- Temporal Perspectives Continuum: stakeholders view synthetic media as accelerated evolution of historical manipulation, but speed creates unprecedented regulatory urgency (p. 9; pp. 14–15).
- Technical binary solutions (detection, watermarking) are limited alone; disclosure must move "beyond the 'AI or not' binary" to describe the creation "recipe" (pp. 10–11).
- Voluntary guidelines build consensus/evidence base but "lack enforcement ('teeth')" and cannot replace formal regulation (pp. 11–12).
- Interpersonal trust over corporate incentives is required to overcome defensive corporate positions (pp. 12–13; pp. 15–16).

## 4. DEFINITION
- **Multistakeholder Process**: "...one in which there are many stakeholders working together in a learning process and towards a common goal... agreements are based on cooperation, stakeholders consciously deal with power and conflict, and there are both bottom-up and top-down strategies integrated into governance and policymaking" (Section 1.2, p. 3, citing ICANN).
- **AI Governance**: "...policies, norms, standards, regulations, and technological interventions both within and beyond government" (Section 1.1, p. 2, citing Carnegie Council).
- **Synthetic Media**: "...images, videos, and audio clips, that [have] been significantly altered or generated by algorithms, including by AI," further specified as "highly realistic, would not be identifiable as synthetic to the average person, and may simulate artifacts, persons, or events" (Introduction, pp. 1–2).

## 5. CITABLE
> "...locking anybody into a particular approach, a particular technology doesn't make sense. Because no matter what the technology and people's understanding of the technology is now, it will change, and so policies become irrelevant very quickly" (quoting Industry Stakeholder PAI8; Section 4.4.3, p. 13)

> "They are not effective at motivating action of any kind, let alone collective action. They are very helpful for understanding the motivations of people who sign up for voluntary actions... but without any teeth, it's very difficult" (quoting Industry Stakeholder PAI13; Section 4.4.1, p. 12)

> "...trust happens over time [and when it builds to] sufficient capacity, then you can focus on solving problems, not taking corporate positions." (quoting Media Stakeholder PAI9; Section 4.4.2, p. 12)

## 9. LIMITATIONS
- Breadth over depth: broad ambitious overview sacrifices deep analysis of individual issues (power dynamics, specific policy interventions, distinct social impacts) (p. 20 in transcript numbering).
- Stakeholder selection bias: the 13 PAI interviewees were already embedded in a voluntary collaborative framework, biasing toward valuing multistakeholder models (p. 21).
- Geographical/Western bias: North American/European jurisdictions only, a "distinctly Western viewpoint" (p. 21).
- Author positionality: researcher is a full-time PAI employee and directed both case studies, creating potential structural bias and "may have impacted the willingness to ask difficult questions" (p. 22–23).
