## 1. WHY
AI autonomous decision-making in finance/healthcare/law enforcement challenges traditional legal accountability, fault and negligence principles built on identifiable human actors. Reactive legal systems struggle with the adaptive, opaque "black box" nature of AI, risking an "accountability gap" that threatens human rights, legal certainty, and public trust.
*Location: Introduction, pp.11–13.*

## 3. WHAT
- Lifecycle-spanning accountability: design, development/training, implementation, post-deployment evaluation. *(p.15)*
- Multifaceted transparency obstacles: technical complexity, trade-secret protection, no global consensus on explanatory standards. *(pp.16–17)*
- Human-centric bias prevention requiring multidisciplinary intervention + "meaningful human oversight." *(pp.19–20)*
- Distributed/proportional liability model across developers, data providers, corporate users, operators. *(pp.21–22)*
- Adaptive, risk-based regulatory paradigm — dynamic, interdisciplinary, cross-jurisdictional. *(p.23)*

## 4. DEFINITION
"Conceptually, algorithmic accountability can be defined as the principles and mechanisms that require algorithmic systems, particularly autonomous or semi-autonomous ones, to be held ethically, legally, and socially accountable for their decisions and the resulting impacts (Atoum, 2025)." *(p.14)*

## 5. CITABLE
