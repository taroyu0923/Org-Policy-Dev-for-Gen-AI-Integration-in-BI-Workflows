## 1. WHY
Generative-AI use by employees outside approved governance ("Shadow AI") is spreading; the authors argue it inherits the risks of Shadow IT and adds new ones (privacy, bias, hallucination, opacity), and that rigid IT control policies lag behind day-to-day practice. *Location: Abstract p.1; Introduction pp.1–2.*

## 2. HOW
Empirical mixed-methods design.
- Survey: n = 140 IT professionals and executives (finance, education, healthcare, technology; roles "ranging from analysts to senior managers"), about 620 invited, 22.6% response, live 8 weeks, printed as "(e.g., November 2024 to December 2024)". *(§5, p.6)*
- Interviews: **eight** semi-structured interviews, February 5–25, 2025, 30–45 min (average 41), 18 transcript pages, five at the World Economic Forum in Davos; guideline pilot-tested with three executives. *(§4 and §6, p.6; Table 1, p.6)*
- **Internal inconsistency:** the Abstract (p.1) and Introduction (p.2) say **10** executives; the body (pp.6, 9–11) and Table 1 say **eight**. Cite eight (the table supports it) and flag the discrepancy.
- Survey is not split by role or sector and does not identify BI, analytics or reporting roles.

## 3. WHAT
- **Governance drift zone** (conceptual contribution, see §4). *(§10, pp.11–12)*
- **Perception–policy gap in survey means (1–7 scale):** aware of an approval requirement 4.4; transparency 4.0; training received 3.2 against importance 5.8; productivity impact 5.3; usefulness 5.2; job replacement 62.9% no / 27.1% maybe / 10% yes. *(§7.1–7.5, pp.7–8)*
- **Executive testimony on bans:** five of the eight executives said ChatGPT is explicitly forbidden; four of the eight said their organisations officially support Shadow AI (Microsoft Copilot). *(§6, p.6; §7.8.3, p.9)*
- **Controlled enablement** as the recommended posture, with four numbered recommendations and five "urgent measures". *(Table 2, p.12; §12, pp.12–13)*

## 4. DEFINITION
"Shadow AI—defined as the next generation of Shadow IT adapted to AI-driven tools and methods—" and "the unsanctioned use of AI systems outside approved governance frameworks." *(Abstract, p.1)*

"Our study identifies a 'governance drift zone': a condition in which policies exist, but fail to align with the informal, emergent nature of AI tool use. This zone is neither fully regulated nor entirely anarchic—it is a gray space where informal AI practices persist with minimal organizational resistance." *(§10, pp.11–12, spans the page break)*

## 5. CITABLE
The source speaks mainly from the executive side; the survey reaches practitioners but asks about awareness and attitude, not about their own bypass.
- "employees can still access ChatGPT and other AI apps on their personal devices, even if we block them internally." Interviewee I5, in a sentence introduced by "executives admitted that it is difficult to prevent employees from using non-approved AI tools". *(§7.8.3, p.9)* Executive testimony about employees, not employee self-report.
- "a dynamic, negotiated process embedded in informal routines and organizational culture." (governance reconceptualised as "not … a top-down control function"). *(§10, p.11)*
- "Foster cross-functional AI governance committees (IT, HR, Legal, Ops) to adapt policies dynamically based on frontline tool adoption and feedback loops." *(Table 2, p.12)* A recommendation, not a finding.
- **Do not cite** the "official tools as misaligned with their job requirements" sentence (§2.4, p.4) as a Silic et al. finding: it is the authors reporting Walters (2021) about Shadow IT.

## 9. LIMITATIONS
Verbatim, PDF-checked. NotebookLM's list mixed verbatim text with paraphrase; only these are kept.
1. "the qualitative interviews were conducted with senior executives, many of whom were engaged during the World Economic Forum. This sampling context, while rich in strategic and policy-level insight, may reflect a form of elite bias—emphasizing perspectives from digitally mature, globally connected organizations. As such, our findings may underrepresent challenges faced by mid-level managers or employees in less resourced or highly regulated sectors." *(§13, p.13)*
2. "although the mixed-methods design strengthens the study by integrating both contextual and behavioral perspectives, it also introduces interpretive asymmetries. While qualitative interviews surfaced nuanced governance dilemmas, the quantitative survey was necessarily limited by the framing of predefined categories." *(§13, pp.13–14)*
3. "executive interviews conducted during the World Economic Forum may skew toward elite, innovation-focused perspectives. While these views provide valuable governance insights, they may underrepresent challenges faced in smaller or less-resourced settings." *(§3, p.5)*
4. "While convenience sampling carries the risk of bias, efforts were made to diversify the sample across industries, organizational roles, and geographic locations." *(§5, p.6)*
Also stated (future-research framing): "future work could also address bias and access gaps by incorporating perspectives from employees with limited digital agency or those in non-technical roles." *(§13, p.14)*

## 11. WORKING-TIER RECEPTION
**Verdict: substantive on reception and gap, documented absence on feedback channels.** Working-tier data comes from the 140-person survey, executive testimony, and recommendations. **Confirmed positive by Albert, Sep 29, 2026** (counts as not-absence in the §11 tally; denominator 35 → 36), with sub-question (d) a documented absence.
- **(a) How policy reaches practitioners.** Training rated 3.2/7 against 5.8/7 importance; awareness of the approval requirement 4.4/7; transparency 4.0/7 (this rates how AI is used internally, not documentation). Executives describe network blocks paired with an approved tool (Copilot). Informal channels are named only as "informal routines" (p.11); no data on them.
- **(b) Gap between stated policy and practice.** The governance drift zone (§4). Bypass evidence rests on executive statements (I5, p.9; §8.2, p.11: "Employees continue to use unauthorized AI tools despite formal restrictions"), not on respondent self-report.
- **(c) Practitioner attitudes.** Productivity 5.3/7, usefulness 5.2/7, job replacement 10% yes / 27.1% maybe / 62.9% no. No "check-the-box" behaviour is reported.
- **(d) Channels to influence policy.** **Documented absence.** Only recommended (anonymous usage logs, reporting channels, cross-functional committees; Table 2 p.12, p.13); none measured.
- **(e) What practitioners do when policy is silent.** Not observed separately. The paper does not distinguish use *before* any rule existed from use *around* an existing rule (relevant to claims K5 and K11), and its survey items do not ask respondents about their own bypass.
- **Scoop-or-position for K5:** **position, not scoop.** B9 is the first source in the corpus with primary empirical data on unauthorised AI use, so K5's "no source observes shadow use directly" now holds only for the other 35 notes. It observes it through executives and awareness items, at population level, with no BI unit of analysis, no critical incidents, no before-rule/around-rule split and no upward-feedback measure.
