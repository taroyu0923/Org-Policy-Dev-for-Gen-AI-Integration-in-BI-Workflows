## 1. WHY
Work on sensemaking and sensegiving had focused on top-level strategy; the paper looks at how middle managers do it in daily routines and conversations ("a so far ignored third-order explanation", p.1432). *Location: introduction p.1413; pp.1414–1416.*

## 2. HOW
Exploratory single-case study "of the ethnographic type" (p.1418): "During a six-month period, I spent four days a week in the enterprise" (p.1418); a clothing company with "sales of about $6 million (Canadian) and around 200 employees" (p.1418); two middle managers (sales manager and collection manager, p.1418); "In total, 39 actions were identified" (p.1424). Analysis questions: "What is the meaning of this action for the actor?", "What are the underlying social rules?" (p.1424).

## 3. WHAT
Four micro-practices (p.1424): translating the new orientation; overcoding the strategy; disciplining the client; justifying the change. The paper links disciplining and justifying to issue selling: "recall the mechanism of issue selling documented in the literature on middle managers" (p.1435). Overview of practices in Table V (p.1433).

## 4. DEFINITION
"Sensemaking has to do with the way managers understand, interpret, and create sense for themselves based on the information surrounding the strategic change. Sensegiving is concerned with their attempts to influence the outcome, to communicate their thoughts about the change to others, and to gain their support" *(p.1415)*; sensemaking and sensegiving are "two sides of the same coin" *(p.1415)*.
"Strategic sensemaking and sensegiving processes are the result of a complex set of mundane micro-practices embedded in tacit knowledge and social contexts" *(p.1433)*.

## 5. CITABLE
The paper is about a clothing firm; the sensegiving is directed at outside clients.
- "success or failure in strategic change depends on how managers interpret and enact the new orientation during their interactions and conversations" *(p.1414)*
- "Translating involves telling people the stories they want to hear and comprises two superposed actions" *(pp.1424–1425)*
- "everyday interactions and conversations matter as much as formally orchestrated events or strategy documents when it comes to change" *(p.1433)*
